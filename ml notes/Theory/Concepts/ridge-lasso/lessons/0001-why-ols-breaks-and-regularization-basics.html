Why OLS Breaks — and How Regularization Fixes It

Before memorizing L₁ and L₂ formulas, nail the exact failure mode of Ordinary Least Squares, the bias–variance trade, and the two preprocessing rules that trip up half of ML candidates.

~7 min read + drills Win: Explain WHY we regularize, scale, and exclude β₀

1. Where Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Breaks

Standard linear regression chooses coefficients β to minimize the Residual Sum of Squares (RSS):

RSS(β) = ‖y − Xβ‖₂² = Σᵢ (yᵢ − β₀ − Σⱼ xᵢⱼβⱼ)² Closed-form OLS estimator: β̂OLS = (XᵀX)⁻¹ Xᵀy  ·  Estimator variance: Var(β̂OLS) = σ² (XᵀX)⁻¹

By the Gauss–Markov theorem, OLS has the lowest variance among all unbiased linear estimators.[ISLR Ch.6] Yet in real datasets, "lowest among unbiased" can still be catastrophically bad in three situations:

  1. High-dimensional / small-sample regime (p ≈ n or p > n): When predictors approach or exceed rows, OLS fits noise with near-zero training error. When p > n, XᵀX is singular (not invertible), so infinitely many solutions give RSS = 0. Why it hurts test MSE: Zero bias on training data, massive variance on unseen data.
  2. Multicollinearity (correlated features): Suppose x₁ and x₂ are nearly identical (e.g., house area in sq ft and number of rooms). You can set β₁ = +1000 and β₂ = −998 and get nearly the same training predictions as β₁ = 1, β₂ = 1! Why mathematically: Near-collinear columns make the smallest eigenvalue of XᵀX nearly zero, so (XᵀX)⁻¹ blows up and Var(β̂OLS) = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹ explodes.[Hoerl & Kennard 1970]
  3. Many irrelevant noise features: Every useless column absorbs random noise from y, adding roughly σ²/n to test prediction variance without improving true signal.

2. The Regularization Trade: Buying Lower Variance with Bias

Expected test prediction error decomposes into three pieces:[ESL Ch.3]

E[(y₀ − ŷ₀)²] = Bias(ŷ₀)² + Var(ŷ₀) + σ² OLS insists on Bias = 0. Regularization accepts a small Bias² to slash Var(ŷ₀).

How regularization works: Add a non-negative penalty on coefficient size to the loss, controlled by a hyperparameter λ ≥ 0:

Ridge (L₂): minβ { RSS(β) + λ Σj=1..p βⱼ² } Lasso (L₁): minβ { RSS(β) + λ Σj=1..p |βⱼ| } When λ = 0 → OLS. As λ → ∞ → all slopes β₁…βₚ shrink to 0 (constant mean model ŷ = ȳ).
Why penalizing large β works (the core intuition) Wild, opposing weights like +1000 and −998 have a huge penalty (1000² + 998² ≈ 2×10⁶), whereas moderate weights like +1 and +1 cost almost nothing (1² + 1² = 2). The penalty forces the optimizer to prefer small, stable weights unless the data strongly justifies larger ones.

3. The Two Non-Negotiable Preprocessing Rules (and WHY)

Interviewers love asking about preprocessing before even touching calculus. Memorize both rules with their proofs:

Expand each rule for the step-by-step "How" and "Why"

Rule 1: Always standardize features before Ridge or Lasso

How: Transform every predictor to zero mean and unit variance: x̃ⱼ = (xⱼ − μⱼ) / sⱼ (using training-fold statistics only).

Why: In OLS, multiplying a feature by 1000 (e.g., measuring income in thousands of dollars instead of dollars) simply multiplies its estimated coefficient by 1000, leaving predictions xⱼβⱼ completely unchanged (OLS is scale-equivariant).

In Ridge and Lasso, that invariance breaks: a feature measured in kilometers instead of meters has a 1,000× larger coefficient, so Ridge penalizes it 1,000,000× more and Lasso 1,000× more![ISLR §6.2.1] Standardizing puts every predictor on an equal footing so the penalty reflects predictive strength, not arbitrary units.

Rule 2: Never penalize the intercept β₀

How: Notice the penalty sum runs over j = 1 … p, deliberately omitting j = 0. Equivalently, if you center each feature (mean 0) and center y, the optimal intercept is always β̂₀ = ȳ.

Why: Adding a constant +100 to every target value yᵢ (e.g., converting Celsius to Kelvin) should just shift every prediction by +100. If you penalized β₀, the penalty would pull β̂₀ toward zero, systematically under-predicting high-mean targets and making predictions depend on the arbitrary origin of y.[ESL §3.4.1]

Bonus Edge Case: What about one-hot encoded categorical variables?

Why interviewers ask this: If you standardize a rare binary dummy variable (e.g. 1% ones, 99% zeros, s ≈ 0.1), dividing by 0.1 inflates the "1" entry to +10, making its coefficient 10× smaller and therefore less penalized than a 50/50 dummy!

Practical answer: For binary/dummy columns, either leave them unscaled (or scale continuous variables by 2s, Gelman's convention) and check via cross-validation; also remember that with regularization you should usually keep all k one-hot columns instead of dropping a reference category, because dropping one breaks symmetry across categories under a penalty.

Interview Oral Drill · 60 seconds

Interviewer asks: "My OLS model has low training error and huge weights like +4,200 and −4,195 on two features. Why is that happening, and why does adding a penalty fix test error even though it adds bias?"

Speak your answer aloud first, then reveal the grading rubric
  • Diagnose the symptom: Large opposing weights are the classic signature of multicollinearity — the two features move together, so their difference carries near-zero variance, inflating Var(β̂) = σ²(XᵀX)⁻¹.
  • Invoke Bias–Variance: Total test MSE = Bias² + Variance + σ². OLS enforces Bias = 0 at the cost of massive variance.
  • Explain the fix: Penalizing ‖β‖₂² or ‖β‖₁ rules out canceling mega-weights; the tiny induced bias is outweighed by a dramatic variance drop, lowering test MSE.
  • Bonus signal: Mention standardizing predictors first and leaving β₀ unpenalized.
